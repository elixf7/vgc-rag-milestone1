## Utility Moves — Role in VGC

In doubles formats, nearly every competitive Pokémon runs between one and three utility moves — moves whose primary purpose is support rather than dealing damage. Utility is defined by intent: a move counts as utility when its main goal is something other than raw damage output. For example, **Surf** on a weak Special Attacker used to proc a partner's **Weakness Policy** is a utility move, even though it deals damage. Understanding utility moves is foundational to both teambuilding and in-game decision-making.

## Utility Moves — Move Category Overview

The game categorizes moves as Physical, Special, or Status. Most utility moves are Status-category, but some damage-dealing moves function as utility because of their secondary effects. Examples include **Breaking Swipe** (lowers both opponents' Attack by one stage) and **Dragon Tail** (forces an opposing switch, useful as Trick Room counterplay).

## Protect — Core Utility Move

**Protect** is the single most important utility move in VGC and belongs on nearly every Pokémon that is not holding a **Choice Item** or **Assault Vest**. It allows a Pokémon to stall a turn while teammates act, enabling:
- Scouting opponent moves and items.
- Buying time for speed control (**Trick Room**, **Tailwind**) to resolve.
- Keeping a Pokémon alive while its partner handles a threat.
- Denying momentum to the opponent on a given turn.

Because **Protect** has almost no viable alternatives for these purposes, it is the default fourth move on most sets and the baseline when evaluating whether a utility slot is needed.

## Speed Control — Utility Moves That Manipulate Turn Order

Speed control — manipulating which Pokémon move in which order — is one of the most impactful team-level decisions in doubles. Several utility moves provide this:

- **Trick Room**: Reverses speed order for five turns; slower Pokémon move first. Best on dedicated teams built around low-Speed Pokémon.
- **Tailwind**: Doubles the Speed of the user's side for four turns. Enables fast, frail sweepers to outspeed threats they normally couldn't.
- **Icy Wind** / **Electroweb**: Deal damage and lower the target's Speed by one stage. Useful because they combine chip damage with speed control and can spread across the field in some cases.

Speed control moves define the tempo of a game. Having access to more than one form of speed control — or ways to disrupt the opponent's — is a strong structural advantage.

## Redirection — Follow Me and Rage Powder

**Follow Me** and **Rage Powder** redirect all single-target attacks toward the user for that turn, completely shielding the partner from those moves. The two moves are functionally identical except that **Rage Powder** does not work against Grass-types or Pokémon holding **Safety Goggles** or with the **Overcoat** ability.

Effective redirection users:
- Have high defensive stats to absorb the redirected hits.
- Carry disruptive moves (**Spore**, paralysis moves) so they threaten the opponent even when not redirecting.
- Use **Protect** to create a difficult decision for the opponent: hitting the partner risks the redirect, hitting the redirector risks a **Protect**.

Redirection is strongest when paired with a Pokémon that would otherwise be extremely vulnerable to opposing spread or targeted attacks — setup sweepers, fragile wallbreakers, and restricted legendaries all benefit from this protection.

## Fake Out — Priority Flinch Utility

**Fake Out** is a priority move that always flinches the target but only works on the first turn a Pokémon enters the field. It effectively wastes one of the opponent's Pokémon's turns, trading one of your own attacks for one of theirs.

Key usage notes:
- Only use it when the trade benefits you — flinching a Pokémon that was going to use **Protect** anyway is wasteful.
- Best users are bulky and have a pivot move (**U-turn**, **Parting Shot**, **Volt Switch**, **Eject Button**) so the Pokémon can re-enter and use **Fake Out** again.
- Alternatively, fast, frail Pokémon holding a **Focus Sash** can use **Fake Out** effectively as a one-time play.
- Flexible across all game stages — can be used offensively to enable a KO or defensively to stop an opponent's key move.

## Taunt — Limiting Opponent Options

**Taunt** prevents the target from using Status-category moves for three turns, shutting down Trick Room setters, redirection users, recovery moves, and sleep moves. Critically, **Taunt** also prevents **Protect**, removing one of the most reliable defensive tools available.

**Taunt** is most valuable against support-heavy teams. It works best on fast Pokémon that can move before the target, ensuring the lock lands before the opponent can act. It simplifies your own game plan by narrowing what the opponent can do each turn.

## Status Conditions — Utility via Affliction

Inflicting a status condition can cripple an opponent's Pokémon for extended stretches. The five permanent status conditions and their strategic value:

- **Sleep**: Most impactful — incapacitates the target for one to three turns. Inflicted via **Spore** (highest accuracy), **Sleep Powder**, or **Hypnosis** (lower accuracy). Even low-accuracy sleep moves are worth considering given the payoff.
- **Burn**: Consistently valuable; halves the target's Attack stat, severely weakening physical attackers. Applied with **Will-O-Wisp**.
- **Freeze**: Extremely powerful when it procs, but triggered only as a low-percentage secondary effect and therefore unreliable as a strategy.
- **Paralysis**: Reduced in value since **Thunder Wave** no longer works on Electric-types, Speed reduction dropped from 75% to 50%, and accuracy dropped to 90%. Still situationally useful.
- **Poison / Toxic**: Not recommended as a primary strategy — VGC games typically end before passive poison damage accumulates to a meaningful advantage.

Status moves are situational and require dedicated move slots. Prioritize **Spore** or **Will-O-Wisp** over other status options given their reliability.

## Screens — Reflect, Light Screen, Aurora Veil

**Reflect**, **Light Screen**, and **Aurora Veil** reduce incoming damage to two-thirds (⅔) of its original value in double battles for five turns (eight with **Light Clay**). This is less than the halving in singles and affects only the user's side of the field.

Strategic considerations:
- Screen setters often do their primary job (getting screens up) and then faint, leaving a 3-vs-4 scenario. The remaining Pokémon must justify the numbers disadvantage through the damage reduction they receive.
- **Light Clay** is near-mandatory for dedicated screen strategies to extend duration to eight turns, but it commits the Pokémon's item slot and often one to two move slots, reducing its versatility.
- Screens are disproportionately weak to critical hits, which bypass the damage reduction entirely.
- Most effective when paired with Pokémon that need extra turns to set up or that would otherwise be one-shot by spread moves.

## Stat-Dropping Moves — Debuffing the Opponent

Lowering an opponent's stats disrupts their offensive plan without requiring a KO. Common options:

- **Icy Wind** / **Electroweb**: Speed drops that double as speed control (see above).
- **Fake Tears**: Lowers the target's Special Defense by two stages, setting up large Special damage from a partner.
- **Eerie Impulse**: Lowers the target's Special Attack by two stages, defanging special attackers.
- **Breaking Swipe**: Lowers both opponents' Attack by one stage simultaneously, effective against physical-heavy teams.

Stat-dropping moves pair best with offensive partners positioned to exploit the debuff immediately. Without a follow-up threat, the opponent can simply switch to clear the drops.

## Setup Moves — Boosting Your Own Stats

Setup moves raise a Pokémon's own stats, usually for an offensive sweep. Setting up is inherently risky in doubles because both opponents can target the Pokémon during the vulnerable turn. Once boosts are accumulated, the threat level increases sharply.

Two main categories:
- **Large single-stat boosts**: **Swords Dance** (+2 Attack), **Nasty Plot** (+2 Special Attack), **Belly Drum** (+6 Attack at the cost of half HP). Best on Pokémon with natural priority or that already outspeed most threats.
- **Multi-stat boosters**: **Dragon Dance** (+1 Attack, +1 Speed), **Quiver Dance** (+1 Special Attack, +1 Special Defense, +1 Speed), **Geomancy** (+2 Special Attack, +2 Special Defense, +2 Speed). These trade immediate power ceiling for broader boosts including Speed.

Standard set structure for a setup sweeper: **Protect** / STAB 1 / STAB 2 or coverage / Setup Move.

Setup sweepers require protective support — **Fake Out** and redirection (**Follow Me** / **Rage Powder**) are the most effective ways to give a Pokémon a safe turn to boost.

## Recovery Moves — Sustaining Bulky Pokémon

Recovery moves (**Recover**, **Roost**, **Slack Off**, etc.) restore lost HP mid-game. They are best on naturally bulky Pokémon where most neutral hits deal 40% or less — if the Pokémon is too fragile it will be KO'd before it can use the recovery move to meaningful effect.

Examples of Pokémon well-suited to recovery moves: **Porygon2**, **Dusclops**, **Milotic**. These Pokémon often serve as long-term attrition tools, chipping away at the opponent while repeatedly recovering lost HP.

## Anti-Setup — Haze, Roar, and Phazing Moves

When opponents accumulate stat boosts rapidly, anti-setup tools become essential.

**Haze**: Removes all stat changes from all Pokémon on the field — both opponents' boosts and your own debuffs. Best used on extremely bulky Pokémon (**Tapu Fini**, **Dusclops**) that can survive hits from a boosted attacker and reset them. Note that **Haze** also clears your own positive stat changes, so avoid using it when your team is boosted.

**Roar**, **Whirlwind**, **Dragon Tail**, **Circle Throw**: Force a switch on the target, resetting their stat changes. These moves almost always go last in the turn order. Key use: forcing a switch before a **Trick Room** setter can act (since **Trick Room** has the lowest priority). Limitations: fail against Dynamax Pokémon; dedicated Trick Room teams typically carry **Fake Out** or redirection to neutralize phazing attempts.

## Manual Weather — Sunny Day and Rain Dance

Permanent weather is most reliably set by passive weather-setting abilities (**Drought**, **Drizzle**, etc.). Manual weather moves — primarily **Sunny Day** and **Rain Dance** — are niche options that let a team flip the weather after the opponent's weather ability has already activated. This is most powerful on dedicated weather teams that want to contest opposing weather setters after they switch in.

## Ally Switch — Position Swapping

**Ally Switch** swaps the positions of the user's two Pokémon, causing any moves aimed at one to instead hit the other. It functions primarily as a mind-game tool — its value is highest when the opponent does not know it is present, forcing difficult targeting decisions.

Weaknesses:
- Has no effect if the opponent guesses correctly.
- Countered by spread moves, smart target selection, and hard reads.
- Loses value quickly once the opponent knows it is in the set.

If an opponent is using **Ally Switch**, the most reliable answer is eliminating the user before it can generate difficult end-game scenarios rather than trying to play around it perfectly.

## Moveset Construction — General Framework

As a general template, most competitive Pokémon sets follow this structure:

- **Protect**
- STAB move 1
- STAB move 2 or coverage move
- Utility or additional coverage

A Pokémon's role determines how much this shifts. Fast, frail sweepers (e.g. **Urshifu**) often prefer two to three attacks. Dedicated support Pokémon (e.g. **Cresselia**) often run three utility moves and one attacking move. The key question when evaluating a utility move: *is the primary purpose of this move to deal damage, or does it have another goal?* If the latter, it's utility — and it earns its slot by the support it provides, not the damage it adds.
