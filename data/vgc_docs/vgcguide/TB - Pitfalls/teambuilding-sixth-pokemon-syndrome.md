## Teambuilding — Sixth Pokémon Syndrome

"Sixth Pokémon Syndrome" describes a common teambuilding trap: the final roster slot has accumulated so many required roles that no single Pokémon can fill all of them. The symptom is cycling through last-slot candidates repeatedly without any feeling right.

The core insight is that Sixth Pokémon Syndrome is not actually a problem with the sixth Pokémon — it is a problem with the first five. When the existing core has too many unaddressed weaknesses, no single addition can fix them all. Recognizing this reframes where the real work needs to happen.

## Teambuilding — Diagnosing Sixth Pokémon Syndrome

The condition is only present after genuinely exhausting reasonable options for the last slot. Before diagnosing the problem as structural, confirm that a broad range of candidates has actually been tested. Reaching toward Pokémon with very low representation in the current metagame is often a signal of over-extension — it suggests the core's requirements have become too narrow for conventional answers.

Signs the problem is structural rather than a missing candidate:
- Multiple last-slot Pokémon have each solved some problems but created or left others unaddressed.
- The list of "must-haves" for the final slot has grown to three or more distinct roles.
- Testing keeps producing the same recurring weaknesses regardless of which Pokémon fills the slot.

## Teambuilding — Three Paths Out

When Sixth Pokémon Syndrome is confirmed, there are three actionable responses:

**1. Accept the team's flaws and continue.**
Every team has weaknesses — no roster covers everything. If the remaining weaknesses are narrow (e.g. a poor matchup into a single uncommon team structure, or an occasional ladder loss), they may not be worth restructuring for. Proximity to an event also affects this calculation: as time runs short, accepting known flaws and developing counterplay strategies is often more productive than rebuilding. If the team is otherwise well-understood and the player is comfortable with it, the sixth slot should not become a reason to abandon the whole project.

**2. Rework a larger portion of the team.**
When flaws are more fundamental, the honest response is stepping back to restructure. Options include:
- Scaling down to two or three Pokémon the player considers essential, then rebuilding around that smaller core from a fresh angle.
- Identifying subtler synergies or elements from the current team worth carrying forward into a new build, even if the overall team is abandoned.

**3. Abandon the team entirely.**
In some cases the team cannot be salvaged efficiently. This is a legitimate outcome — the process of building and testing the team still produced metagame knowledge, Pokémon-specific experience, and a clearer sense of what works and what doesn't. That knowledge transfers to the next project even when the team itself does not.

## Teambuilding — When to Accept Flaws vs. When to Rebuild

The decision between accepting flaws and rebuilding comes down to two variables: how serious the weakness is, and how much time remains before the next event.

- **Serious weakness + ample time**: rebuild or rework.
- **Serious weakness + little time**: develop in-game counterplay for the matchup; rebuilding creates more uncertainty than it resolves.
- **Minor weakness + any time**: accept it and continue; every team concedes something.

Being weak to a dominant, widely-played team structure is generally worth addressing. Being weak to an uncommon or niche strategy is usually not worth restructuring the whole team to fix.
