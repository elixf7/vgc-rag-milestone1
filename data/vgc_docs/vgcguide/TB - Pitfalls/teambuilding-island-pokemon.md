## Teambuilding — Island Pokémon

An "Island Pokémon" is a Pokémon that can be safely ignored by the opponent without consequence. Because Doubles always has two Pokémon per side, an opponent who has no reason to target a specific Pokémon can simply direct all attacks at its partner — winning consistent 2v1 situations until the island Pokémon is left alone and eliminated at the end. The term captures how the Pokémon ends up isolated, watching from the sideline as its teammates are picked off one by one.

The diagnostic question for any roster candidate: **can my opponent safely ignore this Pokémon?** If yes, it is likely an Island Pokémon and should be reconsidered.

## Teambuilding — Board Presence and Why Doubles Demands It

In Doubles, each Pokémon needs to exert **board presence** — an immediate or near-immediate impact on the game state. Board presence takes two forms:

- **Offensive pressure**: the Pokémon threatens meaningful damage, forcing the opponent to acknowledge it.
- **Supportive pressure**: the Pokémon protects a partner, redirects attacks, applies status, or sets up a threat the opponent must address soon.

Without one of these, there is nothing preventing the opponent from ignoring the Pokémon entirely while attacking its partner. The partner faint removes the benefit of the 2v2 structure entirely.

**Why purely defensive strategies from Singles do not translate to Doubles:** a Pokémon that absorbs damage and heals in Singles can win by outlasting the opponent's single threat. In Doubles, that same Pokémon's partner is always exposed. The opponent does not need to break the defensive Pokémon — they only need to eliminate its partner and then exploit the resulting 2v1. Stalling strategies and pure walls are therefore generally ineffective in Doubles.

## Teambuilding — Defensive Pokémon That Are Not Islands

Not all defensive Pokémon are Island Pokémon. A defensive Pokémon avoids island status by retaining a form of pressure the opponent cannot ignore:

- **Status application** (e.g. a **Rage Powder** / **Spore** user like **Amoonguss**): ignoring it risks redirection of attacks or sleep on a key Pokémon.
- **Attack redirection** (**Follow Me** / **Rage Powder**): ignoring it allows it to protect the partner indefinitely.
- **Chip damage that accumulates** (e.g. **Night Shade** + **Pain Split** on **Dusclops**): slow but consistent damage that cannot be dismissed without cost.
- **Setup that becomes a win condition**: a Pokémon that uses setup moves to become independently threatening (e.g. stat-boosting into a high-damage attack) forces the opponent to address it or lose to it. These strategies typically involve sacrificing support Pokémon to create a difficult late-game scenario.

The common thread is that ignoring the Pokémon has a concrete cost — the opponent cannot simply redirect all resources elsewhere and expect no consequence.

## Teambuilding — Setup Strategies as an Exception

Some defensive Pokémon that would otherwise be islands can function as win conditions through setup. Two illustrative archetypes:

**Guard Split + Minimize / Defense Curl (e.g. Chansey):** The team's goal is to use support Pokémon to raise the setup Pokémon's defenses dramatically, then sacrifice those supports and attempt to win a 1v4 with the remaining Pokémon. This strategy exerts extreme pressure once established — the opponent cannot ignore the setup Pokémon. The significant downside is vulnerability to critical hits, which can end the strategy immediately.

**Iron Defense + Body Press (e.g. Ferrothorn):** A Pokémon that boosts its Defense stat simultaneously improves both its durability and its offensive output through **Body Press**, which calculates damage using Defense. When paired with a Pokémon that can accelerate the Defense boosts, this creates a threat that must be addressed quickly. Teams without the coverage typing or move category to efficiently damage it can be overwhelmed before they find an answer.

Both archetypes share the same key property: once set up, the opponent can no longer safely ignore the Pokémon. The setup phase, however, is the window of vulnerability — the strategy typically requires partner support to survive long enough to become threatening.

## Teambuilding — Evaluating Any Roster Candidate

Before finalizing a Pokémon on a team, apply two questions:

1. **What is this Pokémon doing?** It should have a clear answer — offensive pressure, support, speed control, win condition, or some combination.
2. **Can my opponent safely ignore it?** If a reasonable opponent would route all resources toward the partner without concern, the Pokémon is likely an Island.

Every Pokémon on a team does not need to deal large amounts of damage, but every Pokémon does need to be an active participant in the game state. Passive presence is not participation.
