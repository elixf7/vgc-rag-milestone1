## Pressure — The Simultaneous Move Structure of Pokémon

Unlike most turn-based games, Pokémon battles require both players to input their moves at the same time, without seeing what the opponent has chosen. Neither player knows for certain what the board state will look like after the turn resolves. Decision-making must therefore be based not on what will happen, but on what is threatened — what each player could do, and what the consequences of each possible action are. This structure is the foundation of competitive Pokémon strategy.

## Pressure — Definition

Pressure is the threat of proactive action. A Pokémon applies pressure to opposing Pokémon when it threatens to take a meaningful action — dealing significant damage, securing a knockout, inflicting a status condition, or establishing a field condition — that would advance the attacking player's position. The Pokémon being threatened is under pressure and may need to react.

A Pokémon can apply pressure to one opposing Pokémon specifically, or to both simultaneously. The broader and more immediate the threat, the greater the pressure.

## Pressure — Proactive vs. Reactive Actions

All in-game actions fall into one of two categories:

- **Proactive actions** advance a player's goals directly: dealing damage, taking knockouts, setting up speed control, applying status conditions, or establishing offensive positioning for future turns. Proactive actions fail only when they do not achieve their intended outcome (a knockout is not secured, a move misses, etc.) — they have no inherent strategic downside.
- **Reactive actions** are responses to a specific proactive threat: switching out, using **Protect**, or using a recovery move. Reactive actions are necessary when an opponent's threat is severe enough that failing to respond would result in a significant setback or loss.

Because both players move simultaneously, the decision of whether to act proactively or reactively in a given turn depends on correctly reading which threats are most immediate and most consequential.

## Pressure — How to Identify It

Assessing the pressure relationship on the field each turn is the core decision-making process. Useful questions:

- **Who is moving first?** Speed determines which Pokémon acts before others have a chance to react. The faster Pokémon often applies pressure first, and the threat of its most likely action shapes what the slower Pokémon's trainer can reasonably do.
- **Which Pokémon are threatening knockouts?** A threatened knockout is the most immediate form of pressure and usually forces a reaction.
- **Can both opposing Pokémon combine to secure a knockout?** The combined damage output of both opposing Pokémon may threaten a knockout that neither could achieve alone.
- **Are any Pokémon threatening disabling status moves?** Moves like **Sleep** or **Paralysis** can restrict future actions significantly and may demand a preventive reaction even when a knockout is not immediately threatened.
- **What does the next turn look like?** Project forward: if every Pokémon on the field accomplishes its primary goal this turn, which player is in a stronger position next turn? Working backward from that projection identifies which Pokémon is applying the most consequential pressure right now.

When multiple sources of pressure exist simultaneously and both players have viable proactive options, prioritize identifying the most severe immediate threats and the actions most likely to be taken.

## Pressure — Using Pressure to Read Opponents

Pressure is not only a guide to one's own decision-making — it is also a model of the opponent's thought process. A player under pressure has fewer viable proactive options and is more likely to make reactive moves. A player who is not under pressure has more freedom to act proactively.

By mapping the pressure relationship from the opponent's perspective — what are they threatened by, what is their safest path to a proactive move, what reactive options are available to them — it becomes possible to anticipate which moves they are most likely to make. This reduces uncertainty about what the board will look like after the turn resolves, and improves the quality of decisions made before it does.

## Pressure — Predictions

A prediction is a decision made by fully committing to a specific action based on the existing pressure relationships and an assessment of how the opponent will respond to them. Predictions are rooted in reactive reasoning — they require modeling the opponent's likely response to the current position — but the resulting action may itself be proactive. When multiple proactive actions are available, a prediction identifies which is best given the opponent's most probable move.

## Pressure — Core Principle

In Pokémon, no action is made independently of the opponent's. Every move is made in the context of what the opponent could do, and every decision the opponent makes is shaped by what your Pokémon threaten. Keeping the pressure relationship — who threatens whom, and how immediately — at the center of in-game decision-making is the foundation of consistent competitive play.
