## Typing — Defensive vs. Offensive Framing

A Pokémon's typing affects both how it takes damage and how it deals damage. In teambuilding discussions, **typing** usually refers to the defensive dimension — how a Pokémon holds up against incoming attacks. Offensive contribution from typing is discussed separately in terms of **STAB** (Same Type Attack Bonus) and **coverage moves**. Keeping these two concepts distinct helps clarify what role a Pokémon's type plays on a team.

## Typing — What Makes a Typing "Good"

Typing is always evaluated in context. A typing is strong when it resists attacks that are common in the format being played. Indicators of good defensive typing include:

- Few weaknesses overall, or no 4x weaknesses to frequently used attack types.
- Weaknesses that exist are to move types that are difficult to fit onto competitive teams (e.g., **Bug-type** and **Poison-type** moves are rarely run in most formats, making those weaknesses less exploitable).
- The typing synergizes with teammates — resisting attacks that threaten other Pokémon on the team, enabling switches and improving overall durability.

A Pokémon's typing is only useful if its bulk is sufficient to make use of it. Good defensive typing on a Pokémon with very low defensive stats may not translate into practical switch-in opportunities.

## Typing — What Makes a Typing "Bad"

A Pokémon is hindered by its typing when it has many weaknesses, few resistances, or a 4x weakness to a frequently used attack type. Poor defensive typing limits when a Pokémon can be switched in, which is exploitable by opponents. If a Pokémon with bad typing is on the team for offensive reasons, the typing weakness may be manageable — but the constraint on switching will be noticeable in games and requires planning around.

## Typing — Steel-Type as a Defensive Example

**Steel-type** is frequently valuable defensively because of the large number of attack types it resists. Classic examples of Steel-types that combine good typing with sufficient bulk include **Metagross**, **Bronzong**, and **Dialga**. However, not all Steel-types can be used this way — **Kartana** and **Bisharp**, despite being Steel-type, have significant holes in their defensive stats and important 4x weaknesses that make defensive switching unreliable. As a result, these Pokémon are typically built and played for offensive contribution rather than defensive utility. **Kartana** is sometimes given an **Assault Vest** to patch its Special Defense and expand its switch-in opportunities against non-Fire-type attacks. **Bisharp** more commonly runs a **Focus Sash** or **Life Orb** and is used for its offensive coverage and ability rather than its Steel typing.

## Typing — Stakataka as a Case Study in Defensive Typing

**Stakataka** illustrates how a Pokémon with strong defensive typing and bulk can dominate a matchup when paired with appropriate support. Its Steel/Rock typing, combined with good physical bulk, lets it wall a wide range of threats. When supported by teammates that block or redirect the few moves that can threaten it, **Stakataka** can be extremely difficult to remove. Key support interactions that amplify defensive typing include:

- **Wide Guard** blocking spread moves that might otherwise hit it for significant damage.
- **Misty Terrain** from **Tapu Fini** preventing status moves (e.g., **Spore**) that would neutralize it.
- **Desolate Land** from **Groudon** negating Water-type attacks.

The lesson: a Pokémon with good typing becomes dramatically more effective when the team actively covers its remaining vulnerabilities.

## Typing — Typing in Teambuilding: Coverage Is Contextual

A common misconception is that a team must cover every type offensively (able to hit every type for super effective damage) or defensively (able to resist every attack type). In practice, type coverage is contextual — the Pokémon a team will face most frequently determines which types matter most to cover. Priorities:

- Identify the most common attacking threats in the format and ensure the team has Pokémon that can switch into them.
- Avoid stacking multiple Pokémon with the same weakness to a common attack type, as this creates exploitable patterns.
- Some type weaknesses (e.g., to rarely used move types) are acceptable to leave uncovered.

Using a team composition tool to visualize defensive weaknesses and cross-referencing the output against format knowledge is a practical approach for catching major gaps.

## Typing — Typing Should Not Drive Selection Alone

Selecting a Pokémon primarily because its typing fills a gap — rather than because the Pokémon itself is strong and useful — is a warning sign. This approach tends to lead toward Pokémon with weak offensive output, which are easy to justify based on supportive or defensive qualities but struggle to contribute in games against high-damage threats. Ideally, a Pokémon earns a spot because of what it does offensively or supports teammates in doing, and its typing is a secondary benefit that improves team synergy. When the search for perfect defensive typing dominates the building process, it is often a signal that the core of the team needs rethinking.

## Typing — Doubles vs. Singles: Key Difference

Pokémon that are strong defensive pivots in single battles may not translate to doubles. In doubles, a Pokémon cannot safely switch in based on its typing alone, because the opposing second Pokémon may threaten it with a different attack type. **Skarmory** is a well-known example: its high physical Defense and good defensive typing make it effective in singles as a counter to physical attackers, but in doubles, the opponent's partner Pokémon can target it with special attacks, removing the safety of the switch-in. The increased pace of doubles matches and the reduced relevance of entry hazards further limit the viability of Pokémon whose value in singles depends on those mechanics.

## Typing — Typing in High-Power Metagames

In formats built around very high-damage restricted Pokémon, the type chart becomes more narrowly focused. When a small number of Pokémon hit substantially harder than the rest of the field, resisting their attacks takes priority over broad type coverage. Under these conditions, a team can reasonably deprioritize coverage against less common types and instead focus on surviving and handling the dominant threats. The practical implication is that "covering the whole type chart" matters less than identifying and answering the specific high-priority threats in the format.
