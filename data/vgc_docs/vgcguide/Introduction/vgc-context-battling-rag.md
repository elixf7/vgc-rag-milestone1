## VGC Battling — Context and Situational Matchups

In VGC, no Pokémon "beats" another unconditionally. Every matchup is shaped by the specific battle state it occurs in: which Pokémon are active alongside each combatant, what moves they carry, what speed-control mechanics are in effect, and what prior damage has been dealt. Type advantage is a starting point for evaluating a matchup, not a conclusion.

When assessing whether a Pokémon handles a given threat, a thorough analysis considers:

- **Move coverage**: Does the threat carry a move that reverses the expected type interaction? A Pokémon with a type advantage may still lose if the opposing Pokémon carries a coverage move targeting its weakness.
- **Speed and turn order**: Which Pokémon acts first under normal conditions? How does that change under **Trick Room**, **Tailwind**, or **paralysis**? A Pokémon that "wins" the matchup at neutral speed may lose if turn order is reversed.
- **Doubles partner interactions**: What Pokémon is the threat likely to be paired with? A partner using **Fake Out**, **Follow Me**, or **Wide Guard** can completely change how a matchup resolves on a given turn.
- **Frequency of scenarios**: Not all contexts are equally likely. A savvy player weighs how often a given scenario realistically occurs — not just whether it is theoretically possible.

## VGC Battling — Theorymonning

**Theorymonning** (or theorymon) is the practice of mentally simulating battle scenarios during the teambuilding stage. A player who theorymons well can identify potential problems with a team before playing practice games, by walking through likely matchup scenarios and their outcomes.

Theorymonning skills develop through two complementary practices:

1. **Playing VGC games**: Experience builds a library of real scenarios, teaching how Pokémon interact in practice and what opponent decision-making looks like. More games played means a richer mental model to draw from when simulating new scenarios.
2. **Deliberate scenario analysis during teambuilding**: Explicitly walking through how a team handles specific threats, what partners the opponent is likely to use, and which team configurations solve or worsen a given matchup. This is the "thinking" half of theorymon.

The two reinforce each other: practice games provide raw material for better theory, and better theory focuses practice on meaningful scenarios. Theorymonning skill is a learnable muscle — it improves with deliberate, repeated application.

## VGC Battling — Limits of Theorymonning

Theorymonning has a fundamental limitation: a player cannot fully predict how an opponent will approach a matchup. Opponents may find solutions to a team's intended strategy that were not considered during preparation. This means:

- Testing matchups in practice against other skilled players is essential, not optional. Solo theorymonning leaves blind spots that an opponent can exploit.
- The more people a team is tested with, the more likely it is that unconsidered solutions surface before a tournament.
- A matchup that seems acceptable in theory may have straightforward counters that only become visible once an opponent applies fresh eyes.

Collaborative testing — building and theorizing with teammates who will actively try to break the planned strategy — is the most effective way to close theorymon blind spots.

## VGC Battling — Sixth Pokémon Syndrome

**Sixth Pokémon Syndrome** refers to the error of trying to solve too many problems with a single team slot. When a team has a problematic matchup against a specific archetype or threat, the temptation is to find one Pokémon that patches all the weaknesses at once. This tends to fail because:

- A single Pokémon cannot reliably cover a diverse set of threats that each require different answers.
- Overloading one slot with too many responsibilities often means it handles none of them well.
- The opposing archetype may have multiple tools to work around the counter, making a one-Pokémon solution insufficient.

The better approach is to evaluate whether the rest of the team can collectively handle a problematic matchup through positioning, support, and diverse win conditions — rather than expecting one Pokémon to solve it alone. If a matchup is genuinely unwinnable without a dedicated sixth slot, the team structure itself may need revisiting.

## VGC Battling — Countering Pokémon in Doubles

Because VGC is a doubles format, "countering" a threat is rarely about a single Pokémon answering another in isolation. Effective countering in doubles accounts for:

- **The threat's partner**: The most dangerous combination is often the paired duo, not the individual Pokémon. Dismantling a core means addressing the partner that enables it (e.g. the redirection support, the speed setter, the damage amplifier) as much as the primary attacker.
- **Multiple angles**: A robust answer to a threat works across different board states — not just the ideal one. A counter that only functions when certain conditions are met is a fragile solution.
- **Team-wide coverage**: The answer to a difficult matchup is often distributed across multiple Pokémon rather than concentrated in one. Each team member contributes a piece: chip damage, speed control, priority, or defensive utility.
