## Knowledge Base — Why Opponent Modeling Matters

Competitive Pokémon is played with imperfect information: a battle begins with no knowledge of the opponent's full team, moves, items, or EV spreads. The foundation of strong play is the ability to narrow down what an opponent's Pokémon can do before seeing it in action. Players who build a working model of the format's common Pokémon can make higher-quality decisions from turn one, because they are choosing between a smaller set of plausible opponent actions rather than reacting to infinite unknowns.

## Knowledge Base — Where to Start

Learning every Pokémon in a format at once is impractical. A more effective approach is to prioritize the roughly 20 most common Pokémon in the format and build a detailed mental model of each. For each of those Pokémon, the three most strategically valuable things to learn are:

- The moves it most commonly carries.
- The items it most commonly holds.
- How it is generally trained (nature and EV distribution).

Usage data — which this system injects from current format sources — provides the empirical basis for each of these three areas.

## Knowledge Base — Reading Move Usage Data

Move usage rates for a given Pokémon can be divided into three tiers that each call for a different in-game response:

- **High-usage moves** (above ~25% usage): Assume the Pokémon has these moves until proven otherwise. These form the default threat profile when the Pokémon is brought to battle.
- **Moderate-usage moves** (below ~25% usage, but still listed): Do not proactively play around these, but keep them in mind if the opponent is in a position where an uncommon move would give them a decisive advantage.
- **Rare moves** (not reflected in common usage data): Do not cover for these. When a rare move does appear, the correct response is to adapt in the moment rather than having anticipated it.

The 25% threshold is a useful heuristic, not a hard rule. A simpler shortcut is to memorize the top six moves for each key Pokémon. Recall also that a Pokémon can only carry four moves: if an opponent uses a rare or uncommon move, they have likely sacrificed one of their standard options, which can be exploited.

## Knowledge Base — Reading Item Usage Data

Items are fewer in number than moves, but have an outsized effect on how a Pokémon functions. A general rule: cover all items that appear at 10% usage or above. When facing a Pokémon, assume it could carry any of those high-usage items until the actual item is revealed through play. Lower-usage items should be tracked mentally but not actively played around until confirmed.

Item knowledge shapes concrete in-game decisions — for example, knowing whether a Pokémon is likely to hold a **Choice Scarf** (faster but locked into one move) vs. an **Assault Vest** (bulkier, no status moves) vs. a **Life Orb** (raw power, recoil) changes what attacks are safe and what plays are punishable.

## Knowledge Base — Reading EV and Nature Data

EV spread data is more granular than move or item data, and the big picture matters more than any individual spread. When reviewing spread data for a Pokémon, the key questions are:

- How much Speed investment do most builds use? This determines whether the Pokémon is likely to move before or after specific teammates and threats.
- Are there distinct build archetypes (e.g. fast-and-offensive vs. bulky-and-slow) that require different counterplay?
- Is there a common trend across the most-used spreads that suggests a standard training goal?

A working default assumption — e.g. "assume maximum Speed investment until confirmed slower" — lets a player make correct decisions in the absence of confirmed information, then update as the game provides new data.

## Knowledge Base — Adapting to Surprises

Usage data describes the landscape of common choices, not every possible choice. Creative teambuilding is a real part of competitive Pokémon, and unexpected moves, items, spreads, or strategies will occasionally appear regardless of preparation. The goal of building a knowledge base is not to eliminate surprise entirely — it is to ensure preparation for the common cases, so that mental bandwidth during a game can be directed toward adapting to the uncommon ones. The ability to adapt to unfamiliar scenarios is itself a distinct skill that develops alongside format knowledge.

## Knowledge Base — Practical Application During Battle

When a Pokémon is revealed on the opponent's team, a player with a solid knowledge base immediately loads a default threat model: the high-usage moves to respect, the likely item, and the probable Speed tier. As the game progresses and the opponent's choices provide more information, that model is updated. Good in-game decision-making is a process of continuously narrowing the set of plausible opponent options and finding plays that are sound against as many of those options as possible.
