## Cores — Concept Overview

A core is a group of Pokémon whose synergy is strong enough that it defines how the team plays. Core Pokémon typically support each other both offensively — hitting different parts of the type chart, working together to take knockouts — and defensively — switching in on attacks that threaten each other. Identifying a core early in teambuilding is practically useful because it gives direction to the rest of the team: remaining slots are built around supporting the core's strengths and covering its gaps.

## Cores — Fire/Water/Grass Core (FWG)

The Fire/Water/Grass core pairs Pokémon of those three types, each of which resists the super effective attacks that target the other two. The core lends itself to bulky offense: each Pokémon needs to be able to switch in and absorb a hit to fully exploit the defensive synergy, so all three tend toward higher bulk rather than pure frailty.

Strategic value:
- Natural three-way type coverage means the team can pivot between Pokémon to handle a wide range of threats without taking super effective damage.
- Most formats include standout Fire-, Water-, and Grass-type Pokémon, making it relatively accessible to construct.
- Works best when each Pokémon also contributes utility beyond typing — abilities, support moves, or offensive pressure — so the core adds value beyond just switching.

The core is strongest when the three Pokémon also have complementary base stat profiles (e.g., one physical attacker, one special attacker, Speed coverage across slow/mid/fast brackets) rather than overlapping in the same stats or roles.

## Cores — Dragon/Fairy/Steel Core (Fantasy Core)

The Dragon/Fairy/Steel core pairs Pokémon of those three types, which share strong defensive synergy: each resists or is immune to attacks that are super effective against the others. Pokémon of these types also tend to have high base stat totals, which makes it easier to find strong individual pieces.

Strategic value:
- More flexible than the FWG core — even two of the three types often provide strong mutual coverage, so partial versions of this core are viable.
- Dragon, Fairy, and Steel each have good offensive coverage as well, meaning the core can threaten a wide range of opposing Pokémon.
- Pairs well with restricted legendaries and high-BST Pokémon that commonly fall into these types.

When building around this core, the same principle applies as with FWG: look for pieces that contribute more than just their typing — abilities, stat distribution, and move access matter as much as the type triangle.

## Cores — Format Specificity

Many cores are strongest within the format they are built for and lose value in other formats because their specific Pokémon are rotated or the threats they answer are no longer prevalent. When evaluating a core for a given format, the question is not just whether the typing triangle is intact, but whether the specific Pokémon available to fill those roles are strong enough — and whether the threats those types answer are actually common in that format.

Classic cores like FWG and Fantasy provide a structural starting point, but the core's quality ultimately depends on which Pokémon are available and legal. The same type triangle with weaker individual pieces produces a weaker core.

## Modes — Teambuilding Modes

A teambuilding mode is a pair (sometimes three) of Pokémon on the team that work together in a way distinct from the team's core. Two main forms:

- **Lead pair**: Two Pokémon frequently brought together as the intended starting position (e.g., a rain setter and a Swift Swim sweeper form the team's "rain mode").
- **Disjoint mode**: Two Pokémon that synergize well with each other but operate somewhat separately from the rest of the team (e.g., a **Trick Room** setter and a slow sweeper on an otherwise fast team form a "Trick Room mode").

Modes differ from cores in how the team relates to them. A core is what the team is built around — the rest of the team supports it. A mode is something the team can access as an option, but it may not mesh seamlessly with every other Pokémon on the roster.

## Modes — When to Add a Teambuilding Mode

A mode adds depth and variability to a team that already has a solid core. With a core plus a mode, the team gains multiple distinct game plans:

- Play the core as intended.
- Lead with the mode, finish with core Pokémon in the back.
- Lead with core Pokémon, bring the mode in the back for a mid-game momentum shift.
- Flexibly mix core and mode Pokémon across lead and back slots.

Caution when adding a mode:

- **Restricts team preview flexibility**: If one Pokémon in the mode only functions alongside its partner, it must always be brought with that partner. Opponents can identify this dependency from team preview and plan around it.
- **Reduces lead and back options**: Two Pokémon that must be adjacent reduce the number of viable lead and back combinations, making the team more predictable.
- A mode is most valuable when its Pokémon can still function independently or with core Pokémon, so the team retains flexibility even when the mode is not the primary plan.

## Modes — Battling Modes

A battling mode is a recurring game plan — a specific set of Pokémon from the team that work well together in a given scenario and can be reliably deployed across multiple matchups. Unlike teambuilding modes, which are intentional structural decisions during building, battling modes are often discovered through practice.

Battling modes are practical rather than theoretical: the core describes synergy in the abstract during teambuilding; battling modes describe what actually gets brought to games and how those combinations play out in real scenarios.

A team may have multiple battling modes depending on the matchup — a setup-focused mode for slower opponents and an aggressive early-damage mode against frailer teams, for example.

## Modes — Identifying Battling Modes

Questions to identify battling modes on a team in practice:

- Are there matchups where the same four Pokémon always feel necessary?
- Are there pairs that consistently perform better together than with any other combination?
- Are there Pokémon that feel dependent on a specific partner to function effectively?
- After choosing leads, do certain back slots feel obvious most of the time?

Patterns that appear repeatedly across games point to the team's natural battling modes. Naming and recognizing them speeds up decision-making during team preview.

## Cores and Modes — Practical Application

Cores, teambuilding modes, and battling modes are diagnostic tools as much as building tools. When a team feels inconsistent or hard to play, asking structured questions can surface the issue:

- Is the core solid — do the core Pokémon reliably cover each other offensively and defensively?
- Does the mode mesh with the core, or does it create conflicts in which Pokémon can be brought together?
- Are battling modes clear enough that team preview decisions can be made quickly, or does the team lack defined game plans?

Not every team needs all three — some teams function well with only a core and no explicit mode, or with several overlapping modes and no single defining core. These concepts are frameworks for understanding team structure, not requirements.
