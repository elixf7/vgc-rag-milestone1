## Opponent Team Analysis — Overview

At team preview, quickly breaking down an opponent's team into its key components improves both Pokémon selection and in-game decision-making. The goal is to identify the team's primary threats, understand how those threats are enabled, and recognize structural patterns that can be exploited. This analysis draws on format knowledge — understanding how common Pokémon are typically used — and becomes faster and more accurate as that knowledge base deepens. The same analytical framework applies across VGC formats.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Primary Threats

Identify which Pokémon are the team's main sources of damage. These are typically Pokémon with high base Attack or Special Attack, access to powerful STAB or coverage moves, or the ability to boost their own stats (e.g. via **Swords Dance**, **Nasty Plot**, **Iron Defense** into a sweep). Understanding which Pokémon the opponent will be trying to protect and enable is the foundation of the full analysis.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Speed Control

Identify every mechanism the opponent has for manipulating turn order. Common speed control methods include:

- **Tailwind** (doubles the Speed of the user's side for several turns)
- **Trick Room** (reverses turn order for five turns; favors slower Pokémon)
- **Icy Wind** / **Electroweb** (drops opponent Speed stats)
- **Thunder Wave** / **Scary Face** (induces paralysis or heavy Speed drops)
- Priority moves

Assess how speed control interacts with the opponent's primary threats. A key question: which specific Pokémon does a given speed control option enable, and can that Pokémon immediately threaten your team once speed control is established? Pairs like a **Tailwind** setter plus a fast, powerful attacker, or a **Trick Room** setter plus a slow, hard-hitting attacker, should be identified and prepared for.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Team Pace and Playstyle

Evaluate the overall tempo at which the opponent's team is designed to win. Fast, offense-oriented teams aim to apply immediate pressure and close games quickly before the opponent stabilizes. Slower, bulkier teams aim to survive early pressure, establish setup or field conditions, and win in the mid-to-late game. Identifying pace helps predict what the opponent's early turns will look like and whether they will tend toward aggressive or conservative play.

For more detail on identifying pace, see the dedicated pace and playstyle reference.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Raw Speed Tiers

Note the base Speed stats of each of the opponent's Pokémon. Determine which of your own key Pokémon outspeed theirs under normal conditions (no speed control). This tells you which matchups allow you to act first without needing to invest in speed control of your own, and which matchups require it.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Type Coverage

Identify whether any type — particularly one well-represented on your own team — hits the majority of the opponent's Pokémon for neutral or super-effective damage. Conversely, note any types that would resist most of your damage output. If the opponent has a significant type-based weakness, assess whether you have the right Pokémon and moves available to exploit it.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Synergistic Pairs

Look for two-Pokémon combinations that are especially dangerous when both are on the field simultaneously. These pairs typically share a clear division of roles — one enables and one threatens — or share overlapping offensive coverage that makes both hard to answer with a single switch-in. Identifying these pairs before the game helps prioritize which of the opponent's Pokémon to separate or eliminate early.

Strong pairs to watch for include: a speed control setter paired with a Pokémon that immediately capitalizes on that speed control; a redirection user paired with a setup sweeper; or two Pokémon whose combined coverage leaves few safe answers.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Support vs. Offense Ratio

Count how many of the opponent's six Pokémon are primarily offensive versus primarily supportive. Teams that lean heavily offensive may be frail and susceptible to being outpaced if their early aggression is absorbed. Teams that lean heavily supportive may struggle to close games if their key setup is denied. An imbalanced ratio in either direction is often an exploitable structural pattern.

Support Pokémon are generally those whose primary role is not dealing damage — redirectors, **Trick Room** setters, speed control users, and defensive pivots.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Item Reads

For each of the opponent's Pokémon, consider which items are most commonly run on that Pokémon in the current format (based on usage data injected by this system). Note any cases where two Pokémon on the same team commonly run the same item — only one of them can actually have it, and determining which informs how to play against each. Item knowledge shapes decisions around whether a Pokémon can be one-shot, whether a status move will be absorbed, whether a Choice-locked Pokémon is easier to play around, and similar questions.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Applying the Framework

The following scenarios illustrate how these categories combine into a practical read:

**Hyper-offense team**: A team with four or more strong attackers, minimal support, and no **Trick Room** is almost certainly trying to overwhelm the opponent before they can stabilize. Expect an early, aggressive opening. Their Pokémon are likely to be frail, making them susceptible to being picked off if the first few turns can be survived. If one of the primary attackers requires speed control to be threatening, identify and target that enabler early.

**Trick Room team**: A team with multiple potential **Trick Room** users and slow, powerful attackers is oriented around reversing turn order. Expect them to lead with a **Trick Room** setter and a support Pokémon that protects it (e.g. a redirector or **Fake Out** user). When denial is impractical — because they have multiple setters and strong protection options — consider whether reversing **Trick Room** after it goes up, or stalling it out, is more viable than trying to prevent it entirely.

**Balanced offense with speed control**: A team with one or two primary attackers, a **Tailwind** or **Electroweb** user, and defensive support is looking to establish a speed advantage and then trade favorably. Identify which Pokémon are dangerous under their preferred speed condition and whether denying that condition (by eliminating the speed control user early) changes the matchup significantly.

## Opponent Team Analysis — Limits of Analysis

Pre-game analysis is probabilistic, not certain. Opponents may carry unexpected moves, items, or EV spreads that deviate from common patterns. The goal is not to predict every possible scenario but to prepare for the most likely ones and develop a working model of the team's primary threat vectors. Accurate analysis of the common cases frees up in-game attention for adapting to the exceptions.
