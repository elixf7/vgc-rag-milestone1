## Accuracy vs Power — The Core Trade-Off

Many Pokémon have access to both a high-accuracy, lower-power version of a move and a higher-power, less accurate version covering the same type and role. Common pairings:

- **Thunderbolt** (90 BP, 100% accuracy) vs. **Thunder** (110 BP, 70% accuracy outside of rain)
- **Flamethrower** (90 BP, 100% accuracy) vs. **Fire Blast** (110 BP, 85% accuracy)
- **Scald** / **Surf** (80–90 BP, 100% accuracy) vs. **Hydro Pump** (110 BP, 85% accuracy)
- **Dragon Pulse** (85 BP, 100% accuracy) vs. **Draco Meteor** (130 BP, 90% accuracy)

Because these moves are often available to the same Pokémon, the choice between them is a recurring teambuilding decision that directly affects how much variance the team accepts.

## Accuracy vs Power — Why Accuracy Matters at Scale

A single miss in one game may feel like bad luck. Across a full tournament — which can run 20–30 games or more — the cumulative effect of inaccurate moves becomes statistically significant. A move at 90% accuracy misses in 10% of uses; if hitting it is the difference between winning and losing, that translates to roughly 2–3 expected losses over 30 games from that move alone, even if it is only used once per game. Running multiple inaccurate moves compounds the risk further. Because tournament results depend on accumulated wins rather than any single game, minimizing inaccuracy across the field reduces long-run variance in standings.

## Accuracy vs Power — Setting an Accuracy Threshold

A practical approach is to establish a personal accuracy threshold — a minimum accuracy below which a move will not be used unless there is a specific justification. The threshold is a baseline for decision-making, not a rigid rule, and should reflect how much variance the player is willing to accept in exchange for increased power.

Common starting point: treating 90% accuracy as a soft floor — willing to run 90% accuracy moves reluctantly, resistant to going lower without strong justification.

Justified exceptions where dipping below a threshold makes sense:

- **The Pokémon already runs the more accurate version alongside it**: Carrying both **Draco Meteor** and **Dragon Pulse** on the same set provides a fallback, making the inaccurate move less consequential to miss in a given turn.
- **No viable substitute exists**: Some Pokémon have no good alternative to an inaccurate move. The move must be accepted as a built-in risk (see below).
- **The move is not often the primary win condition**: Low-accuracy moves that are not relied on to close games — but provide a large swing when they land — carry lower effective risk. **Will-O-Wisp** (85% accuracy) or a situational **Sleep Powder** can fall into this category when used selectively.
- **A high-risk, high-reward playstyle is intentional**: Some players accept more variance in exchange for higher ceiling damage. This is a valid preference, but it increases the number of games lost to misses.

## Accuracy vs Power — When Inaccuracy Is Unavoidable

Some Pokémon have no practical alternative to an inaccurate move, typically because their primary offensive type has only one viable move in their learnset at that power level.

**Rotom-Wash** is a clear example: its only Water-type attacking move is **Hydro Pump** at 85% accuracy, and the Water typing is a core reason to use the Pokémon both offensively and defensively. There is no accurate substitute, so running **Rotom-Wash** means accepting inherent accuracy risk.

**Groudon** (when legal) faces a similar situation with **Precipice Blades** (110 BP, 85% accuracy, hits both opponents). There is no spread Ground move of comparable power available. Running a weaker accurate alternative (**Stomping Tantrum**) or a different coverage move sacrifices the primary offensive function. Partial mitigation strategies include: being selective about when **Precipice Blades** is clicked — defaulting to the more accurate fire move when a miss would not cost the game — and accepting that some variance is built into the Pokémon's use.

When a Pokémon cannot avoid inaccuracy, the best approach is to minimize the number of turns where hitting the inaccurate move is the only path to winning, and to use more accurate alternatives whenever the game state permits.

## Accuracy vs Power — Practical Decision Framework

When choosing between accurate and inaccurate moves in teambuilding, three questions structure the decision:

1. **Is the higher-power move necessary to hit key damage thresholds that the accurate version cannot reach?** If both moves achieve the same practical outcomes (same KOs, same pressure), the accurate move is strictly better.
2. **Is there a viable substitute on the same Pokémon, or in the team's broader damage output?** If the inaccurate move can be backed up by an accurate alternative, the risk is lower.
3. **How often will the team be forced to rely on the inaccurate move to win?** The more frequently a win requires hitting an inaccurate move, the more games will be lost to variance over a long tournament.

Move power and accuracy are one trade-off among many in teambuilding, but the decisions compound across an entire team and across every tournament game played. Erring toward accuracy is a form of variance reduction; erring toward power is a form of ceiling raising. The right balance depends on the Pokémon, the team's other options, and the level of variance the player is willing to accept.
