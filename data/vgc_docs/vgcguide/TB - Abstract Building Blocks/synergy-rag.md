## Synergy — Concept Overview

Synergy describes how well Pokémon on the same team work together — specifically, how well they cover each other's weaknesses and enable each other's strengths. Synergy operates across typing, stats, moves, and abilities, and plays out both offensively and defensively. It is broken into two main categories: **offensive synergy** (how Pokémon complement each other in dealing damage and taking knockouts) and **defensive synergy** (how Pokémon cover for each other's vulnerabilities and enable safe positioning).

These categories are not binary — pairs of Pokémon may have strong offensive synergy, strong defensive synergy, both, or neither.

## Synergy — Offensive Synergy

Offensive synergy describes how Pokémon work together to achieve damage goals. Three core forms:

**Complementary damage**: Two Pokémon threaten different subsets of opposing Pokémon. Together they cover more of the type chart, making it difficult for opponents to switch safely. If having both Pokémon on the field threatens far more targets than either alone, the pair has strong offensive synergy.

**Covering a weakness**: One Pokémon pairs with another specifically to threaten the types or Pokémon that would otherwise wall or counter it. The partner's purpose is to remove the primary attacker's obstacles.

**Overwhelming damage**: Rather than covering different parts of the type chart, both Pokémon stack damage modifiers — **Helping Hand**, stat boosts, abilities — to hit so hard that defensive typing becomes less relevant. The goal is to exceed any defensive threshold the opponent can present.

Offensive synergy also has a speed dimension: threatening damage is only relevant if the Pokémon can act in time to deal it. Speed, priority, and speed control all contribute to whether an offensive pair can execute its plan before being disrupted.

Note: setup enabling is distinct from offensive synergy. A Pokémon using **Surf** to activate a partner's **Steam Engine** or **Weakness Policy** is setting up, not synergizing offensively — all the damage is being dealt by one Pokémon. Offensive synergy means both Pokémon are contributing to the damage output together.

## Synergy — Offensive Synergy Examples

**Primal Groudon + Xerneas** (when legal together) is a classic example. **Xerneas'** Fairy-type attacks are resisted by Poison, Steel, and Fire — all three of which **Groudon** hits for super effective damage. Each Pokémon covers the other's counters: **Groudon** removes Steel-types and other threats that resist **Xerneas**, while **Xerneas** deals massive damage to Pokémon that threaten **Groudon**. The key to this pair's strength is that stopping one typically requires resources needed to stop the other.

**Xerneas + Ferrothorn** (when both are legal) is an example of poor offensive synergy. A boosted **Xerneas** can threaten almost anything that doesn't resist Fairy, but **Ferrothorn** provides no tools to handle the Fire- and Steel-type Pokémon commonly used to answer **Xerneas**. The pair does not expand each other's offensive reach.

## Synergy — Defensive Synergy

Defensive synergy describes how Pokémon cover for each other's vulnerabilities, enabling safe positioning and resource conservation over the course of a game. The key concept is **switchability**: the ability of one Pokémon to switch in safely on attacks that threaten its partner.

Switchability is determined primarily by typing and base stats. Two Pokémon are defensively synergistic if each can switch into super effective attacks aimed at the other without taking prohibitive damage.

A well-known structural framework for defensive synergy is the **type core**: a preset group of typings that naturally cover each other. Classic examples include the Fire/Water/Grass core and the Fairy/Dragon/Steel ("Fantasy") core. These cores provide a reliable starting point for building defensive coverage.

**Primarina + Ferrothorn** illustrates the concept clearly: **Primarina** can switch into Fire- and Fighting-type attacks that threaten **Ferrothorn**, while **Ferrothorn** can switch into Electric-, Grass-, and Poison-type attacks that threaten **Primarina**. Each Pokémon patches the other's most common vulnerabilities.

Defensive synergy is not limited to typing. Pokémon with very high natural bulk (**Dusclops**, **Porygon2**) can fill defensive roles without relying on type resistances, using raw stats and recovery instead. Conversely, a Pokémon with poor defensive stats has weak defensive synergy with most partners regardless of typing, because it cannot safely absorb attacks.

## Synergy — Defensive Synergy in Doubles

In double battles, two Pokémon are on the field simultaneously, so defensive tools that protect the whole field — not just the user — are especially valuable. **Intimidate** (lowers both opposing Pokémon's Attack), **Reflect**, and **Light Screen** all benefit the entire team's side and improve the defensive position of both Pokémon on the field at once.

Redirection (**Follow Me**, **Rage Powder**) is a defensive tool but is distinct from defensive synergy. **Amoonguss** using **Rage Powder** to protect **Xerneas** is enabling setup, not forming a defensive synergy between the two. **Amoonguss** has genuine defensive synergy with many Pokémon because of its Grass/Poison typing — it can switch into attacks that threaten those Pokémon and apply offensive pressure with **Spore**.

## Synergy — Anti-Synergy

Anti-synergy describes cases where Pokémon on the same team actively interfere with each other's effectiveness. Common forms:

- **Terrain conflicts**: A Pokémon that relies on **Misty Terrain** to prevent status conditions (e.g., **Tapu Fini**) has anti-synergy with a Pokémon that needs to inflict sleep via powder moves (e.g., **Amoonguss**) — **Misty Surge** blocks **Spore**.
- **Weather setter redundancy**: Running multiple Pokémon with conflicting passive weather abilities (e.g., **Drought** and **Drizzle**) means each overwrites the other, reducing both to unreliable options.
- **Priority and terrain conflicts**: **Prankster** Pokémon or those relying on priority moves have anti-synergy with **Psychic Terrain** setters (**Indeedee**, **Tapu Lele**), since **Psychic Terrain** blocks priority moves targeting grounded Pokémon.

Some anti-synergy is acceptable on a team if the benefits of both Pokémon outweigh the occasional conflict (**Tapu Fini** + **Amoonguss** is a common example). The more central the conflicting moves or abilities are to each Pokémon's function, the more problematic the anti-synergy becomes.

## Synergy — Format and Metagame Dependence

Which synergies matter most changes with each format. The types that are required for defensive coverage vary based on which Pokémon are common — some formats make a Steel-type nearly mandatory, while others make it optional. Similarly, format-specific prevalence of certain abilities (**Intimidate**, **Spore**) or strategies (**Trick Room**, spread moves) will shift which defensive and offensive pairings are most valuable.

When evaluating synergy needs for a specific format, look at teams performing well and identify common structural patterns: shared typings, recurring pairings, universal support moves or abilities. These patterns reflect the synergies the format rewards, and recognizing them makes it easier to identify what gaps a team in progress still needs to fill.
