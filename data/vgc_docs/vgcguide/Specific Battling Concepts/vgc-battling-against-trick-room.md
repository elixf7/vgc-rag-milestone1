## Battling Against Trick Room — The Two Core Questions

When facing a team with a **Trick Room** setter, start from two questions:

1. Can **Trick Room** be prevented from going up?
2. How damaging is it if **Trick Room** does go up?

If the setter can be knocked out before setting **Trick Room**, that is usually the preferred outcome. When that is not possible, the strategy shifts to minimizing the number of turns the opponent can effectively use **Trick Room**.

## Battling Against Trick Room — Stalling It Out

**Trick Room** lasts five turns, but the turn it is set counts toward the total, leaving effectively four turns for the opponent to act under it. The goal is to waste as many of those turns as possible.

**Protect** is the most straightforward stalling tool. If both active Pokémon Protect on alternating turns, up to two of the four offensive turns can be wasted. Frequent double Protecting is exploitable — the opponent can use the free turns for setup moves, switches, or recovery — but it establishes the baseline logic: every turn under **Trick Room** that the opponent cannot use offensively is a turn closer to its expiry.

Other methods of consuming **Trick Room** turns without conceding progress:

- **Smart defensive switches**: Switching in a Pokémon that resists or is immune to the expected attack forces the opponent to either attack into poor matchup or reposition themselves.
- **Status moves**: Using moves that do not directly advance the opponent's offense while still consuming a turn.
- **Fake Out**: Forces a flinch on the targeted Pokémon, consuming its turn without the Trick Room team making progress.

## Battling Against Trick Room — Positioning and the Value of Not Taking KOs

Trick Room teams typically do not lead with their primary sweeper. The sweeper is held in reserve while the setter and a support Pokémon (e.g. a redirector or **Fake Out** user) handle setup. A common pattern: the support Pokémon uses redirection to protect the setter on turn one, **Trick Room** goes up, and the sweeper is brought in with four turns to act.

A key insight: if the support Pokémon is not knocked out on turn one, the opponent faces a difficult decision on turn two. They can keep it on the field — where it is vulnerable to defensive plays and now only three turns of **Trick Room** remain — or switch it out for the sweeper, exposing the sweeper to damage or a knockout on entry. Either option is worse for the **Trick Room** team than the situation where the support Pokémon was knocked out immediately and the sweeper entered freely.

This illustrates a broader principle: **taking a knockout is not always the best play**. When the opponent has led two defensive or support Pokémon, leaving them on the field to waste **Trick Room** turns is often more valuable than eliminating them. Pokémon is, in many ways, a game about forcing the opponent into difficult decisions — and leaving threatening-but-defensive Pokémon alive can generate those decisions.

## Battling Against Trick Room — Planning for When It Expires

Stalling out **Trick Room** does not automatically win the game. Before it expires, identify:

- What conditions need to be met to prevent a second **Trick Room** from being set? Which opposing Pokémon must be removed, and which of your own Pokémon must survive?
- Which Pokémon on your team are best positioned to win once **Trick Room** ends and speed order returns to normal?

Planning for the post-**Trick Room** game state while the field effect is active is what separates successfully stalling **Trick Room** from actually winning the game.

## Battling Against Trick Room — Implications for Hyper Offense

**Trick Room** is a primary structural weakness of hyper offense teams — teams built entirely around offensive capability with minimal defensive tools. Executing the defensive positioning required to stall **Trick Room** (protective switches, Protecting strategically, avoiding giving the opponent clean turns) requires Pokémon and moves that hyper offense teams typically do not carry. This makes hyper offense teams more vulnerable to **Trick Room** opponents and is a meaningful consideration when building for formats where **Trick Room** is common.
