## Teambuilding Intent — What It Is and Why It Matters

Every teambuilding decision — adding a Pokémon, changing a moveslot, adjusting an EV spread — is made for a reason. That reason is called **intent**. Intent is the backbone of the teambuilding process: it is the explicit or implicit justification behind every choice. Intentions can be simple ("**Incineroar** provides strong utility support") or highly specific ("8 Speed IVs on **Cresselia** so it moves before minimum-Speed restricted legendaries under **Trick Room**"). The goal is not to over-engineer every detail, but to be able to articulate and accept the reasoning behind each decision made.

## Teambuilding Intent — Reasons to Start a New Team

Common and valid starting points for building a new team include:

- **Pokémon or mechanic interest** — Building around a Pokémon or combo that is interesting or exciting to use. Example: pairing **Groudon** with **Gravity** to guarantee **Precipice Blades** accuracy.
- **Perceived strength** — Identifying a Pokémon or archetype as particularly powerful and constructing a team to maximize it. Example: building a team centered on a dominant restricted Pokémon.
- **Metagame countering** — Targeting popular archetypes or common Pokémon with a team specifically designed to beat them. Example: building a team with **Durant** to beat a field heavy with **Grimmsnarl** and **Togekiss** support structures.

## Teambuilding Intent — Reasons to Add a Specific Pokémon

When slotting a Pokémon onto a team, the reasoning typically falls into one of these categories:

- **Personal preference or track record** — A Pokémon the builder plays well or has had success with.
- **Role strength** — The Pokémon provides strong, widely applicable utility (e.g., **Incineroar** for **Intimidate**, **Fake Out**, and **Parting Shot**).
- **Defensive synergy** — The Pokémon covers weaknesses of existing teammates. Example: **Arcanine** covering Fire and Grass weaknesses for a core of **Tapu Fini** and **Kartana**.
- **Weakness coverage** — The team has a type or archetype weakness, and this Pokémon patches it. Example: **Togekiss** to handle Dragon-type threats.
- **Enabler role** — The Pokémon specifically sets up another Pokémon to succeed. Example: **Comfey** activating a **Weakness Policy** on **Yveltal** reliably.

## Teambuilding Intent — Using Intent to Evaluate Team Performance

Tracking intent is essential for diagnosing whether problems in games are caused by play errors or by team construction issues. During and after battles, intent provides a clear evaluative framework: did this Pokémon do what it was meant to do? If a Pokémon consistently underperforms relative to its intended role, revisiting why it is on the team — or adjusting how it is played — is the appropriate response. Intent turns vague feelings of "this isn't working" into specific, addressable questions.

## Teambuilding Intent — Implicit vs. Explicit Intent ("Slapping On")

When a choice is made quickly by instinct without articulating a reason, it is called **slapping on**. Slapping on is not inherently wrong — an implicit reason still exists — but unclear intent is a signal that a choice may need more thought. This is also why using another player's team or copying EV spreads without context can be difficult: the reasoning behind the decisions is unknown. When intent is unclear, brainstorming what role a Pokémon is expected to fill or what the team needs from it is a productive exercise.

## Teambuilding Intent — Discussing Teams with Other Players

When reviewing or receiving feedback on a team, the most useful questions are intent-based: "Why did you make this choice?" and "What were you trying to accomplish here?" This allows feedback to be targeted — agreeing or disagreeing with the goals, and evaluating whether the decisions serve those goals. Asking these questions benefits both parties: the reviewer learns the builder's thought process, and the builder is prompted to articulate reasoning they may have left implicit. There are no wrong questions in this process.
