## Consistency — Concept Overview

Consistency in VGC means building and playing in ways that produce wins regardless of variation — variation in opponents' knowledge, in their team choices, in their in-game decisions, and in luck. It operates at three levels: teambuilding (reducing reliance on chance from the start), strategy (executing a plan that works even when opponents know it is coming), and play (making decisions that minimize luck's impact turn by turn). Consistency is more of an ideal than a fully achievable state, but pursuing it builds good habits across all aspects of the game.

## Consistency — In Teambuilding

Variance can be reduced before a game begins by choosing Pokémon, moves, and items that work across a wide range of situations rather than only in specific ones. Utility moves that stop opponents' strategies cold — regardless of what those strategies are — are inherently more consistent than reactive or narrow tools.

Examples of consistent utility moves:
- **Fake Out**: Works on the first turn any time the Pokémon enters the field, regardless of matchup.
- **Taunt**: Shuts down status moves, **Protect**, and setup regardless of what the opponent is trying to do.
- **Haze**: Removes all stat changes, countering setup strategies of many kinds simultaneously.
- **Safeguard**: Prevents status conditions for the team, covering sleep, paralysis, and burn proactively.

Examples of less consistent utility moves:
- **Lucky Chant**: Only relevant in the narrow window where a critical hit would otherwise occur — too situational to reliably justify a moveslot.
- **Roar**: Always resolves last and is reactive — if the opponent uses a damaging move instead of the expected setup move, the team takes damage while giving a free switch.

The consistent/inconsistent distinction applies to items and strategies as well. An item that provides value in nearly every game (e.g., **Sitrus Berry**, **Choice Specs**) is more consistent than one that requires a specific opponent action to activate (e.g., **Air Balloon**, which can be preemptively invalidated).

## Consistency — In Strategy

A consistent strategy works regardless of the opponent's knowledge level, preparation, or familiarity with the team — including opponents who have faced the same team recently or prepared specifically to counter it. A strategy that only functions because opponents don't know what it does is a gimmick — powerful in early rounds or against unfamiliar opponents, but unreliable in best-of-three play or deep tournament runs against experienced players.

Questions for evaluating strategic consistency:

**Initial theory:**
- Does the reasoning behind each team decision hold up to scrutiny?

**Ladder testing:**
- Can the team win consistently against ladder opponents?
- Which opposing Pokémon or combinations does the team struggle against, and how frequently will those appear at the intended competition level?
- If the same opponent is faced twice, does the strategy hold up when they know what to expect?

**Best-of-three practice:**
- Does the team perform well against opponents who know nothing about its decisions?
- Does the team perform well against opponents who know the strategy but haven't prepared a specific counter?
- Does the team perform well against opponents who know the strategy and have specifically adapted their team or game plan to beat it?

A strategy passes the consistency test when it can answer "yes" to all three best-of-three questions. Most strategies fall short of this ideal — identifying where they fall short is the useful part of the exercise.

## Consistency — In Play

Turn-by-turn consistency is about finding moves that produce the best outcome regardless of what the opponent does, rather than moves that win only if the opponent makes a specific choice.

Key principles:

- **Lead flexibility**: At team preview, can a lead be chosen that is not clearly disadvantaged against any of the opponent's likely leads? A lead that forces a favorable matchup regardless of what the opponent starts with is more consistent than one that requires guessing correctly.
- **Avoiding luck dependency**: If winning a game requires landing a low-accuracy move, look for an alternate path first. Only rely on luck when no better option exists.
- **Turn-agnostic moves**: On any given turn, can a move be chosen that is at least acceptable regardless of what the opponent does? Moves that lose badly to certain opponent choices introduce variance; moves that are acceptable across all opponent responses reduce it.
- **Closing out games**: When in a winning position, can a path to victory be found that does not rely on the opponent making a mistake or on favorable RNG? The closer to 100% the win rate in the endgame, the more consistent the play.

The **Venusaur** / **Sleep Powder** case illustrates the principle: **Sleep Powder** at 75% accuracy is a significant gamble. Consistency in play means only using it when missing would not cost the game, or when it is the only remaining path to a win — not as a default first option. Minimizing unnecessary uses of low-accuracy moves directly reduces the number of games lost to variance.

## Consistency — Luck and Variance

Luck cannot be eliminated from competitive Pokémon, but its impact can be minimized. The relationship between luck and skill is not that skilled players have better luck — it is that skilled players navigate to positions where luck has fewer opportunities to decide the outcome. Winning a game 90% of the time in the final turns reflects strong play; navigating to endgames that are won 99–100% of the time regardless of RNG reflects the highest level of consistent play. Reviewing any completed game through the lens of consistency — asking where variance could have been reduced — almost always surfaces improvements, regardless of the result.
