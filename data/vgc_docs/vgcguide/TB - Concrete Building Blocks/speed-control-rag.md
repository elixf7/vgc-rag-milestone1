## Speed Control — Role and Importance

Speed control refers to any mechanic or move that alters the order in which Pokémon act during a turn. Because attacking before an opponent can mean their move never resolves (if they are KO'd first), speed control is one of the most structurally important elements of team design in doubles. Secondary effects from opponent moves — flinches, burns, paralysis, freezes — are also less dangerous when your Pokémon acts first and has already dealt its damage before any status lands. A well-designed team will typically include at least two distinct forms of speed control.

## Speed Control — Dynamic Speed Mechanics (Gen 8+)

From Generation 8 onward, Speed order is recalculated in real time during a turn rather than being locked in at the turn's start. This means that if a Pokémon uses a move that changes Speed mid-turn (e.g., **Volt Switch** into a weather setter that triggers **Swift Swim**), the newly active Speed boost applies to that same turn. This "dynamic Speed" system amplifies the value of first-turn Speed control combos and allows partners to coordinate for immediate impact.

## Tailwind — Speed Control Overview

**Tailwind** doubles the Speed of all Pokémon on the user's side for four turns. The turn it is set counts as one of those turns, giving three effective turns for the setter's teammates. **Tailwind** rewards offensive, fast-moving teams that want to maximize damage output across a limited window.

Strategic notes:
- Best set by fast Pokémon or those with **Prankster** (e.g., **Whimsicott**, **Talonflame**), which gives the move increased priority.
- With **Prankster** or high natural Speed, a first-turn **Tailwind** activates immediately under dynamic Speed rules, meaning the setter's partner can already benefit from doubled Speed on that same turn.
- **Tailwind** setters are typically fast and frail with strong offensive pressure or useful support tools, so the team can capitalize on the boosted Speed window.
- **Tailwind** teams are naturally vulnerable to **Trick Room** teams, which invert the Speed advantage.

## Trick Room — Speed Control Overview

**Trick Room** reverses the Speed order on the field for five turns, causing the slowest Pokémon to move first and the fastest to move last. It has -7 priority and therefore always resolves last in the turn it is used, preventing any same-turn Speed tricks with it. **Trick Room** can be used as a full team strategy or as a mode — a secondary option on a team not entirely built around it.

Strategic notes:
- **Trick Room** setters are almost always built with very high bulk to survive the setup turn, since the move must be used while facing two opponents.
- Setup support via **Fake Out** or redirection (**Follow Me** / **Rage Powder**) is the most reliable way to protect the setter on the turn it uses **Trick Room**.
- Because **Trick Room** inverts Speed, it is naturally effective against opponents' speed control — a team under **Tailwind** becomes disadvantaged once **Trick Room** goes up.
- Committal level is flexible: a team can be fully optimized for it (very low Speed sweepers, multiple setters) or simply include it as a disruptive option.

## Speed Drops — Icy Wind, Electroweb, Bulldoze

Speed-dropping moves lower opposing Pokémon's Speed by one or more stages without requiring significant team commitment. The most common are **Icy Wind**, **Electroweb**, and occasionally **Bulldoze**. These moves are low-investment — a single moveslot — but their effect is less durable than field conditions like **Tailwind** or **Trick Room**, since stat drops reset when a Pokémon switches.

When speed drops shine:
- Late game, when opponents are locked in and unlikely or unable to switch, drops accumulate and become very difficult to remove.
- As supplementary speed control on a team that already has a primary method — the redundancy creates resilience.
- On very fast Pokémon, a speed drop can give an immediate benefit to a slower partner on the same turn it is used.
- On extremely bulky Pokémon that can stay on the field for multiple turns, continuously grinding down opposing Speed.

Speed drops can be nullified if the opponent raises their own Speed through items, abilities, or setup moves.

## Speed Boosts — Setup Moves and Items

Raising your own Speed is generally more durable than dropping the opponent's Speed: a KO on a Pokémon with a Speed drop removes the drop, but a KO secured with your own Speed-boosted Pokémon preserves the boost for future turns. This makes Speed boosts structurally similar in value to **Tailwind** — the benefit persists as long as the Pokémon is alive.

Sources of Speed boosts:
- **Setup moves**: **Dragon Dance** (+1 Attack, +1 Speed), **Quiver Dance** (+1 Special Attack, +1 Special Defense, +1 Speed). Distribution is limited, but Pokémon that can use these moves reliably are strong candidates for sweeper roles.
- **Choice Scarf**: Multiplies Speed by 1.5× at the cost of locking the holder into the first move chosen after entering the field. High reward, but creates exploitable inflexibility.
- **Weather abilities**: **Swift Swim**, **Sand Rush**, **Chlorophyll**, and **Slush Rush** double Speed under their respective weather conditions. Very powerful, but entirely dependent on the relevant weather being active and therefore susceptible to weather disruption.

## Priority Moves — Bypassing Speed Order

Priority moves act before all non-priority moves regardless of Speed stat, bypassing every other form of speed control including **Tailwind**, **Trick Room**, and stat modifications. This makes them a universal fallback for acting first.

Trade-offs:
- Priority moves generally deal less damage than equivalent non-priority attacks.
- Strong priority moves are uncommon; weak priority moves are more widely available and best used for picking off low-HP targets or enabling a partner's stronger attack.
- Priority is typically a supplementary speed control tool rather than a team's primary method.

**Grassy Glide** on **Rillaboom** is an example of priority used strategically: under **Grassy Surge**, **Grassy Glide** gains priority, giving **Rillaboom** a strong, typely-relevant move that acts before slower threats and finishes off weakened opponents.

## Natural Speed — Base Stat Advantage

Pokémon with high natural Speed provide speed control by default: before any field effect is active, the fastest Pokémon moves first. This matters especially on the opening turn and during the turns after **Tailwind** or **Trick Room** expire.

Strategic value:
- Forces opponents to spend turns and resources setting up speed control just to match your natural Speed advantage.
- Provides a fallback when the team's speed control has been disrupted or has expired.
- A team with no naturally fast Pokémon is far more dependent on field effects staying active, which creates exploitable windows.

Limitation: teams built entirely around fast, frail Pokémon struggle due to low bulk. Natural Speed is best as one element of a broader speed control plan rather than the sole approach.

## Paralysis — Speed Control via Status

**Thunder Wave** applies paralysis, halving the target's Speed stat. It is situationally useful but has meaningful drawbacks:
- 90% accuracy — not fully reliable.
- Does not work against Electric-types or Ground-types.
- The Speed halving is less impactful than in older generations, where it was a 75% reduction.

Paralysis is best considered an ancillary speed control tool rather than a primary strategy, and typically requires a dedicated moveslot for a narrower payoff than **Icy Wind** or **Electroweb**.

## Quash and Lagging Tail Trick — Niche Speed Control

**Quash** forces the target to move last within its priority bracket for the turn. It requires the user to act before the target, making it most viable on Pokémon with **Prankster** (e.g., **Sableye**). Distribution is limited.

**Trick** + **Lagging Tail**: A **Prankster** user holding a **Lagging Tail** uses **Trick** on the opponent, giving them the **Lagging Tail** (which forces them to always move last within their priority bracket) while stealing their item. This effectively applies a permanent **Quash** to the target. **Prankster** Pokémon (**Whimsicott**, **Grimmsnarl**, **Sableye**) still act first when using **Trick** even while holding a **Lagging Tail**. Considered situational and inconsistent in most formats.

Both of these options are low-consistency and generally not recommended as primary speed control tools.

## Speed Control — Mixing and Matching

Different speed control types are not mutually exclusive, and the strongest teams often layer multiple forms. General principles:
- Having at least two distinct forms of speed control provides flexibility and resilience against disruption.
- **Trick Room** is naturally strong in formats where fast, frail Pokémon are prevalent, inverting the typical Speed advantage.
- **Tailwind**-centric teams may struggle more in formats where opponents are bulky and hard to KO within the **Tailwind** window.
- Mixing **Tailwind** with speed drops or priority moves covers the gaps when **Tailwind** isn't active or has been played around.
- Speed control strategy should fit the team's broader plan: an offensive, fast-paced team benefits from **Tailwind** and natural Speed; a slower, bulk-oriented team may prefer **Trick Room** or **Icy Wind**.
