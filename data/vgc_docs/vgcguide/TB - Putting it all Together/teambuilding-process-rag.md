## Teambuilding Process — Overview

Building a VGC team follows a general five-step process: establishing a core idea, expanding that core with supporting Pokémon, rounding out the team to cover weaknesses and add modes, finishing all details (EVs, moves, items), and testing and iterating. Pokémon are typically numbered in the order they are added to the team — this ordering helps communicate the thought process when discussing a team with others.

## Teambuilding Process — Step 1: The Main Idea

Every team begins with an anchor: a specific Pokémon, combination of Pokémon, or tactic that defines the team's primary goal. At this stage, add the most pivotal Pokémon to the team builder and sketch rough ideas for their moves, items, or EVs without finalizing details.

If starting from a Pokémon or pair: place them in the builder and note what roles they will fill. If starting from a strategy or tactic: identify which Pokémon are best positioned to execute it and add those first.

The goal of Step 1 is to establish the team's foundation — what the team is built around and why.

## Teambuilding Process — Step 2: Expanding the Core

With the anchor in place, add Pokémon that support and extend the main idea. At this stage, focus on how each addition fits the team's goals rather than finalizing spreads or sets. Pokémon are added for one of three main reasons:

**Complementary offense**: A Pokémon that extends the team's offensive reach — threatening types, positions, or Speed tiers that the core does not already cover. Complementary offense is not limited to type coverage; priority, spread moves, and Speed control all count. Note: adding only offensive Pokémon at this stage can push the team toward hyper offense unintentionally; unless that is the intended playstyle, include Pokémon with both offensive and defensive utility.

**Complementary defense**: A Pokémon that improves the team's ability to switch and position safely — through synergistic typing, **Intimidate**, or high base bulk. Defensive additions must still contribute offensively; every Pokémon on the team should be capable of dealing damage, so it can contribute even if it is the last Pokémon standing.

**Support**: A Pokémon that enables the core through **Tailwind**, **Trick Room**, **Fake Out**, **Follow Me**, or other utility. Support Pokémon should still exert some offensive pressure — purely passive support can reduce the team's overall damage output and leave it unable to close games.

Repeat this process until three to five Pokémon are on the team. The goal at the end of Step 2 is a core that could function in battle if details were filled in.

## Teambuilding Process — Step 2B: Knowing When the Core Is Complete

The core is complete when:

- Nothing feels obviously missing from the team's main strategy.
- The team's major weaknesses are addressed or consciously accepted.
- It is possible to imagine how battles will generally play out.
- The founding idea is fleshed out enough to be tested.

A useful exercise: mentally simulate the team against common expected opponents and ask which four Pokémon would be brought. If no clear and comfortable answer emerges for most matchups, the core may need more work.

Watch for additions made primarily to counter a specific strategy rather than to strengthen the main idea. Counter-specific Pokémon are fine as side benefits, but building around reactive counters early leads to incoherent teams with no clear win condition. The core should be strong enough to handle common threats naturally before specific counters are considered.

## Teambuilding Process — Step 3: Rounding Out the Team

With the core established, the remaining slots address weaknesses and add strategic depth. Three approaches for the final one to three slots:

**Add breadth**: Identify popular Pokémon, moves, or team structures that create difficult matchups for the core, then add a Pokémon that addresses those. Ideally this Pokémon covers gaps the core does not already handle — widening what the team can deal with rather than duplicating existing strengths.

**Add depth**: Add a Pokémon that interacts with core Pokémon in a new way, creating an additional mode or option within the existing framework. This often means a Pokémon with utility moves or an ability that unlocks new play patterns with the core Pokémon already on the team.

**Add a mode**: Add two Pokémon that work together in a way distinct from the core — a **Trick Room** pair on an otherwise fast team, or a fast offensive pair on an otherwise slow team. The mode Pokémon should complement each other and integrate reasonably with the rest of the team even if they operate somewhat independently.

If the team hits a wall — no sixth Pokémon seems to fit — revisit earlier steps with different Pokémon. Returning to the drawing board on one slot is normal; the team structure may need adjustment rather than the final slot.

## Teambuilding Process — Step 4: Finishing Details

With all six Pokémon selected, fill in every element that was left rough in earlier steps: EV spreads, moves, items, natures, and abilities. At this stage the first complete draft of the team exists and is ready to test.

Key questions to finalize during this step:
- Does each Pokémon's EV spread support its intended role (Speed tiers, bulk thresholds, damage output)?
- Does each moveset cover what the team needs from that Pokémon in its expected matchups?
- Does each item amplify a strength or address a weakness for that specific Pokémon?

## Teambuilding Process — Step 5: Testing and Iterating

A first draft is rarely the final team. Testing validates or invalidates the assumptions made during earlier steps. Common questions to answer through play:

- Do the matchups that seemed favorable play out as expected?
- Are there matchups or common Pokémon that create consistent problems?
- Are there Pokémon on the team that rarely get brought, or that feel dependent on specific teammates to function?
- Does the pace of the team match the intended playstyle?

Iteration may mean adjusting a single EV spread, swapping one move, changing an item, or replacing an entire Pokémon. If a Pokémon added early seemed essential but is underperforming in practice, it can be changed regardless of its position in the build order. There is no rule that early additions are permanent.

Teambuilding improves with repetition. Each team built and tested — regardless of how it performs — develops the judgment needed to recognize good cores, identify weaknesses earlier, and find the right sixth Pokémon faster.
