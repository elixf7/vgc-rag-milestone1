## EV Spreads — Simple vs. Complex

A **simple EV spread** focuses on a Pokémon's core strengths and is built for general-purpose performance. It typically maximizes two or three stats to make the Pokémon as effective as possible in a wide range of situations.

A **complex EV spread** is built around a specific, precise goal — surviving a particular category of hit, reaching a meaningful Speed tier, or securing a guaranteed knockout under defined conditions. Complex spreads often distribute EVs across many or all stats rather than maxing a few.

Neither approach is inherently superior. What matters is knowing what your Pokémon is capable of in battle and whether the spread supports the role the Pokémon was added to fill.

## EV Spreads — When a Complex Spread Is Worth Building

Consider a more specialized spread when one or more of the following apply:

- **Speed targeting**: The Pokémon needs to reach a specific Speed value to outpace a threatening Pokémon or to function under a speed-control archetype (e.g. creeping a key threat under **Tailwind**, or undercutting a rival in **Trick Room**).
- **Offensive benchmark**: The Pokémon needs to secure a knockout on a specific target with a specific move, while investing remaining EVs in bulk.
- **Defensive benchmark**: The Pokémon needs to survive a specific category of hit (e.g. a strong physical attack from a common threat), while investing remaining EVs in offense.

**Example — defensive benchmark (Sylveon):**
A **Sylveon** spread of 36 HP / 244 Def / 228 SpA with a **Modest** nature and **Wiki Berry** is built to survive a strong physical Steel-type hit that the max-HP variant cannot reliably endure, while preserving as much Special Attack investment as possible. This is a typical complex spread: it trades generalist bulk for a precise defensive threshold, then redirects freed EVs into offense.

## EV Spreads — Pitfalls of Complex Spreads

Complex spreads carry real risks that can make them worse than a simple spread in practice:

- **The benchmark situation may never arise.** A spread engineered to survive a specific hit is only valuable when that matchup actually occurs. If the opposing Pokémon holds a damage-boosting item, uses a boosted stat, or simply isn't present, the spread's trade-offs were wasted.
- **Secondary damage negates the threshold.** Residual damage from sand, chip from **Fake Out**, switch-in chip, or weak spread moves can push a Pokémon below a survival threshold it was built to hit. Surviving one big hit from full health in a vacuum is often less durable than it appears in practice.
- **The spread may change how the Pokémon functions.** Pulling significant EVs from Attack or Speed to meet a defensive threshold can alter a Pokémon's role entirely. If the result doesn't perform as intended in testing, revisit the spread rather than the gameplan.
- **Speed investment cuts can create permanent matchup losses.** When two Pokémon of the same species threaten to knock each other out, the one that moves first wins. A Pokémon that sacrifices Speed EVs for bulk will consistently lose that speed tie or race unless it commits fully to a bulky, under-speed Trick Room role.
- **Mental overhead in-game.** Spreads and their thresholds must be remembered during a live game. Unexpected outcomes — surviving when you expected to faint, or fainting when you expected to survive — cause misplays. Only build benchmarks you can reliably track in your head.

## EV Spreads — When to Start Simple, When to Go Complex

Starting a new team with simple spreads is usually correct. Complex spreads are most valuable after practice games reveal the specific situations a Pokémon repeatedly finds itself in. Building a complex spread around a scenario you haven't encountered yet risks over-engineering for a problem that doesn't exist.

The practical workflow:
1. Build the team with max-max or role-based simple spreads.
2. Test the team in practice games or ladder play.
3. Identify recurring situations where a simple spread falls short — a Pokémon fainting when it should survive, or failing to secure a needed knockout.
4. Target those specific scenarios with a more precise spread, keeping in mind the pitfalls above.

If a simple spread isn't causing problems, there is no reason to complicate it.
