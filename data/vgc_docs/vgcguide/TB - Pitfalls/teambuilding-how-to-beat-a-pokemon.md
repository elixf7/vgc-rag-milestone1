## Teambuilding — Counters, Checks, and Good Matchups

A **counter** to a Pokémon nearly always beats it across a variety of circumstances. A **check** is favored against the target but not reliably so in all situations. Both terms originate in Singles; in Doubles, the concepts are harder to realize in practice because of the additional layer of partner support.

The practical goal when building against a threatening Pokémon is not a hard counter — it is a **good matchup**: a Pokémon that is strong in general, useful across many situations, and happens to be favored against the target threat. A Pokémon added solely to beat one specific opposing Pokémon often becomes dead weight in all other matchups, which hurts team flexibility.

## Teambuilding — Why Doubles Makes Countering Harder

In Doubles, the opponent has tools that make reliable knockouts on a specific Pokémon difficult:

- **Fake Out** and **Follow Me** / **Rage Powder** can interrupt or redirect a planned attack.
- **Protect** delays the threat for a turn.
- **Intimidate** on a switch-in reduces physical damage immediately.
- **Speed control** can flip priority and let the threatened Pokémon act first.
- The opponent can always switch out the targeted Pokémon and attack with its partner instead.

Because of these tools, "walling" a Pokémon (surviving its attacks without threatening it back) is not sufficient. A Pokémon that can take hits but deals damage slowly will simply be ignored while the opponent attacks its partner.

Some Pokémon have a large enough effect on battle that no single Pokémon can reliably neutralize them — addressing them may require careful planning across multiple roster slots or the team structure as a whole.

## Teambuilding — How to Identify a Good Matchup

**Step 1 — Analyze the threat's strengths.** Identify what makes the opposing Pokémon difficult to deal with:
- Strong offensive coverage across multiple types or move categories.
- High defenses or favorable typing that makes it hard to damage efficiently.
- Utility moves (**Fake Out**, **Intimidate**, sleep, speed control).
- An exceptional Speed stat or access to priority.
- Dependence on an external condition (rain, sun, **Trick Room**) that amplifies its power.
- A unique move or playstyle that disrupts normal gameplan.

**Step 2 — Find tools that exploit its weak points:**
- If the threat has poor defenses or a exploitable typing, look for Pokémon that can hit it for high damage, including super-effective attacks or priority moves.
- If the threat relies on utility support (e.g. **Follow Me**, **Intimidate**), verify that the answer still functions when that support is present.
- If the threat depends on a weather or field condition, include ways to disrupt or suppress that condition.
- If the threat is slow or passive, consider **Taunt** or setup Pokémon that ignore its utility and exploit its lack of offensive pressure.
- If the threat is susceptible to status (burn, sleep), **Will-O-Wisp** or sleep-inducing moves may be worth considering.

**Step 3 — Test.** Confirm that the intended solution works in actual practice games, not just on paper.

## Teambuilding — Counter vs. Check: When to Use Each

The choice between a dedicated counter and a more flexible check depends on two factors: how often the threatening Pokémon appears in the current metagame, and how badly the existing core loses to it.

- A **dominant, widely-used threat** that the core consistently loses to may justify a dedicated counter — a Pokémon whose primary role is addressing that matchup.
- A **common but manageable threat** is better addressed by a check — a Pokémon that covers the matchup while remaining useful in other games.
- A **niche or uncommon threat** usually does not justify dedicating a roster slot to it at all.

The right time to look for a counter or check is when a core of four or five Pokémon is otherwise functional but has a clear, recurring losing matchup.

## Teambuilding — Beating a Combination, Archetype, or Full Team

Addressing an entire archetype or team structure is significantly harder than countering a single Pokémon. A team is more than the sum of its parts — the synergy between its Pokémon must be understood in addition to each Pokémon's individual strengths.

Process for building against an archetype:

1. **Analyze each Pokémon individually** using the steps above — what does each one do, and what are its weak points?
2. **Analyze the synergy** — why do these Pokémon work well together? What conditions or combinations do they create that are especially threatening?
3. **Look for a single addition** that exploits the identified synergy while still fitting the existing team. This solution is unlikely to exist but worth exploring.
4. **If no single answer works**, brainstorm two-Pokémon combinations that collectively address the synergy. The frame shifts to synergy against synergy: can a pair of Pokémon with natural chemistry make the opposing archetype's gameplan difficult to execute?

If building a team from scratch with a specific archetype in mind, start from a core that is naturally advantaged against it — lead with structure rather than trying to patch in answers later.

Always test proposed solutions in practice games before treating them as reliable answers.

## Teambuilding — Avoiding Tunnel Vision

Building too specifically against one threat risks weakening the team against everything else. Key checks:

- A Pokémon that only addresses one specific opposing Pokémon is only useful in games where that Pokémon appears — if it does nothing else, it is a liability everywhere else.
- In Doubles, Pokémon need to work flexibly across multiple roles and game states. A roster slot built around a single, narrow purpose rarely earns its place.
- The goal is a team that is naturally advantaged against the important threats in the format — not a team engineered around one specific matchup at the cost of general performance.
