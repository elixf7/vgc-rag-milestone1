## Team Luck — Concept Overview

How much luck affects a player's results is not purely random — it is significantly shaped by teambuilding and in-game decisions. Teams built with accurate moves, reliable speed control, and balanced composition give luck fewer opportunities to decide outcomes. Three primary levers a teambuilder controls are move accuracy, speed (who acts first), and overall team composition.

## Team Luck — Move Accuracy

Every time a move with less than 100% accuracy is used, the team is exposed to a loss from a miss. Prioritizing accurate moves during teambuilding directly reduces how often games are lost to variance.

Practical approaches:
- **Run both the accurate and the inaccurate version of a move**: When a strong but unreliable move is needed, pairing it with a weaker but guaranteed-to-hit alternative on the same Pokémon gives a fallback. For example, a Pokémon might carry both a high-power move with imperfect accuracy and a lower-power move at 100% accuracy for situations where a miss cannot be afforded.
- **Assess reliance before committing**: Whenever a move below 100% accuracy is included, consider how often the team will be forced to rely on it. If hitting it is critical in most games, the accuracy risk is compounded across many turns and many matches.

This is not an argument for always choosing the more accurate move — sometimes a high-power inaccurate move is necessary to achieve key KO thresholds. The point is to be deliberate about accuracy trade-offs rather than defaulting to maximum power without accounting for the variance introduced.

## Team Luck — Speed and Acting First

Moving after an opponent creates consistent exposure to secondary effects — burns from **Scald**, paralysis from **Thunderbolt**, accuracy drops from **Muddy Water**, flinches from **Rock Slide**, and so on. A Pokémon that has already fainted cannot land these effects. Moving first and securing KOs before opponents act is one of the most reliable ways to reduce negative variance.

Speed control tools that help a team act first consistently:
- **Naturally fast Pokémon**: Create speed advantages without requiring setup turns.
- **Choice Scarf**: Provides immediate 1.5× Speed, allowing a Pokémon to outspeed threats it otherwise couldn't.
- **Tailwind**: Doubles the Speed of the whole team's side for four turns.
- **Icy Wind / Electroweb**: Drop opposing Pokémon's Speed, enabling partners to act first.
- **Trick Room**: Inverts speed order, allowing very slow Pokémon to move first.

A team with poor speed control is implicitly accepting increased exposure to secondary effects and critical hits every turn the opponent gets to act. Factoring speed control into the team's structure reduces that exposure systematically.

## Team Luck — Team Composition and Variance

Beyond individual move and speed choices, the overall structure of a team affects how vulnerable it is to luck.

**Overly fast and frail teams**: A single unlucky turn — a critical hit, a flinch, a status — can cause a cascade that costs multiple Pokémon and loses the game. High-speed, low-bulk teams have high variance because each bad outcome has an outsized impact.

**Overly defensive teams**: Sacrificing offensive pressure to build bulk creates a different risk — the longer a game goes, the more opportunities opponents have to land critical hits, status effects, or disruptive moves. A defensive team that cannot close games efficiently is trading short-term safety for long-term variance.

**Over-reliance on a single Pokémon**: Teams structured around one Pokémon handling a key matchup are vulnerable to losing games if that Pokémon is taken out by one unlucky turn before it can fulfill its role. Spreading matchup coverage across multiple Pokémon reduces this single point of failure.

**Terrain and ability control**: Abilities that control the game state — such as **Misty Surge** (**Tapu Fini**) preventing burns, paralysis, freeze, and sleep on grounded Pokémon — actively reduce the opponent's ability to inflict negative RNG on the team. Building in these passive protections reduces luck exposure across an entire game, not just one turn.

The flip side: building around very defensive setup strategies (e.g., **Calm Mind** on **Tapu Fini**) can generate a false sense of security. The longer a Pokémon spends accumulating boosts while taking damage, the more opportunities the opponent has to land a critical hit that bypasses the bulk. Accepted risk of this kind is not inherently bad — it should be a conscious decision with the trade-off understood, not an oversight.

## Team Luck — Applying the Framework

When reviewing a team for luck vulnerability, three questions surface the main risks:

- **Accuracy**: Which moves on this team have meaningful miss rates, and how often will the team be forced to rely on them? Are there more accurate alternatives available?
- **Speed**: Does this team have reliable tools to act before key threats? What happens when it doesn't move first?
- **Composition**: Is the team over-indexed on fragility, defensiveness, or a single Pokémon? What does one unlucky turn cost?

Luck cannot be eliminated, but consistently asking these questions across teambuilding decisions reduces the number of games decided by RNG rather than play.
